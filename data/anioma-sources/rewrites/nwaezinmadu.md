# Nwaezinmadu: The Person Anioma Names Before It Praises

The child is at the door. The mother sends her on an errand, then spits on the ground, points at the wet mark, and says: *ka nfi tu pu asua taa* — let me see you return before this saliva dries.

The child runs.

That detail is recorded from Ibusa, one of the Anioma communities of Oshimili North in Delta State, and it is worth reading twice, because it contains the whole mechanism of the concept in miniature. A standard of conduct has been set, and it has been set in the open, where the ground and the neighbours and the child can all see it. When the child returns, the mother does not say *thank you* first. She says *dalu nwaezinmadu* — thank you, person of good character.

The praise is not for the errand. It is for the character the errand is taken to reveal.

## What the word is made of

*Nwaezinmadu* is the Anioma term for that character. In some quarters of the region it is heard as *nwaezigbonmadu*. Both are built from the same parts: *nwa*, child; *ezi*, good or true; *nmadu*, person. Run together, the compound names a person of good character — a whole human being, as the Anioma say it, in the way that sum of words carries a verdict rather than a description.

Emeka Esogbue, the Anioma historian, records the term and reports that it is indigenous to the area. The attributions in this piece are his where they concern Anioma practice, and they are set out below so a reader can weigh them, or test them.

It is a welcome salutation as much as an assessment. A mother says it to a child. A community says it of a man or a woman whose conduct has held. It is not a title, and no rite confers it. It is conferred by the fact of being seen, repeatedly, behaving well.

## What the maxim says

The most useful check on a claim like that is another source entirely, from another side of the language, and it exists.

The Igbo maxim *ezi afa ka ego* is rendered in a 2020 study by Damian O. Odo, Kingsley I. Uwaegbute and Virginus U. Eze in *Old Testament Essays* as **integrity surpasses wealth** — a good name outweighing money. The same study reports the maxim as a formulation a community uses to discourage the acquisition of wealth by means a person would not want attached to their name.

That is the moral algebra working underneath *nwaezinmadu*. The currency is *afa*, the name, and the name is a communal holding rather than a private asset. The study notes the extension that matters most here: an offence committed for money soils not only the offender but the family and the community that produced him. Honour is held in common, which is why it is defended in common.

## The same idea in the proverb record

The proverb record gathered by Northcote W. Thomas in the Ibo-speaking peoples of Nigeria, published in 1914, holds 631 proverbs, collected across Asaba, Ika, Ubulu, Uku and Ala, with each entry carrying the dialect of the district it came from. Two of them are close to a statement of the principle.

*Omume onye bụ ndụ ya* — a man's deeds are his life, in Asaba speech, and given no restriction in the collection.

*Omume onye bụ mma* — a man's deeds are his beauty, in Owere and Ọnịcha speech. The collector's note explains when it was said: when a girl refused one man and chose a plainer one. Whoever tells that story is telling it about the plain man who had something the other did not.

There is also *Njọ dị n'onye mere* — evil is with the evil doer — in Asaba, and *Otu onye abụghị ala* — one man is not a town — in Ala speech, recorded for use when a man disregarded public opinion.

Those four are not presented as the foundations of Anioma ethics. They are presented as the evidence that an ethics of this shape was being spoken in ordinary Anioma households in the early twentieth century, in the same dialect area where the idea has since been described. The record and the principle agree with each other. That agreement is the finding.

## How the town reads a person

Anioma communities are not unique in believing that conduct matters. They are distinctive in the apparatus built to read it.

**The kindred.** The *umunna* — the lineage group — is where conduct is first observed and first priced. A man's standing is not settled by his own account of himself; it is settled by what his lineage can say of him without embarrassment. This is why the report from *ezi afa ka ego* is that family image is inseparable from personal image. The unit that raises a person is the unit that answers for him.

**Shame.** Shame and guilt are recorded as the direct opposites of the honour on which *nwaezinmadu* rests — engaged when an individual fails the body of honour his community is bound by. A sanction that operates through shame needs no officer. It requires only that people are known, and know they are known.

**The Iwu festival.** In Ibusa the Iwu festival was the annual moment when the reading was done in public. High Chief Ifeanyichukwu Daddi Okeleke, the Mgboko Isiga of Ibusa, recalled in a 2017 interview that Iwu was "a day of reckoning," that people "avoided involving themselves in social vices that could smear their image" because of it, and that those who had committed evil "were usually confronted with their bad deeds." That account is explicit that the fear was social rather than punitive, and that the festival's authority was strongest in the 1970s and early 1980s.

**The red cap.** Among the Ika communities, the *okpu ododo* — the red cap — was not available to whoever could afford one. Esogbue records that a hunter who had killed a particular kind of animal, or a man who had performed the kingship rites, *Ifie Uzu* and *Ichi Ikenga*, might be entitled to wear it. A man who understood that he was not entitled, and did not wear it, was himself an *nwaezinmadu*. The prohibition is the telling part: the system had a way of making restraint visible.

## What it cannot do

The concept's constraint is the constraint of every ethics that runs on reputation. It works among people who must meet again.

The most instructive case in the record concerns the Atlantic world rather than the village. Ebere Nwaubani's study of the political economy of Aboh from 1830 to 1857 reports that in the early 1830s the British traders MacGregor Laird and R. A. K. Oldfield found that no one in the Lower Niger trade other than Obi Ossai of Aboh could command the flow of goods on the river, and that Ossai held his position by strategic location and by marriage alliances with long-distance traders.

Esogbue reads the same reign as a lesson about the reach of the principle: that a king who carried *nwaezinmadu* into dealings with men who were not bound by it found that it could not bind them.

The scholarly literature on the period does not settle the question of Ossai's own understanding of his European counterparts. What it does establish is the asymmetry — one party to a trading relationship working within an ethics of durable reputation, the other within a commercial order arriving with other intentions.

That is the limit. A principle of good conduct governs the person who has accepted it. It has no jurisdiction over the person who has not.

## Where the value is kept

The vocabulary shows where the value was held. *Nwaezi* is recorded by Esogbue among family names unique to Ibusa, with the meaning "good personality." That is the *ezi* of *nwaezinmadu* living in a surname, still borne by families in the town where the mother spat on the ground.

The concept also travels. Esogbue reports that Anioma people extend the term to individuals from outside the community who are judged to merit it. A good name, in this reckoning, is not a closed clan asset. It is portable property, and it is recognised wherever it is found.

A comparison often reached for is Yoruba. *Ọmọlúàbí* is documented as a Yoruba conception of virtue and moral character — variously rendered as well-born, well-bred, of good character, and treated in the scholarship as resting on good conduct, social harmony and integrity. The Igbo-language side has its own terms, including *ezigbo mmadu*, which Comfort Agulanna's article in the *Journal of Pan African Studies* examines as the Igbo concept of a good person. *Nwaezinmadu* is Anioma's name for the same kind of thing, with *nmadu*, the whole person, at the centre.

The Anioma people are the western Igbo of Delta State, occupying nine local government areas and grouped into Aniocha, Ndokwa, Ika and Oshimili. The term is Enuani. Its reach is Anioma, which is reason enough to name it where it is spoken, rather than file it under the general heading of Igbo morality.

## What the concept asks of a record

The mechanism is a closed loop. A person is watched. A person is praised by name. A person is checked by the knowledge that the watching continues and the name is remembered, by their family and by their town.

It is not a system of detection. It is a system of attention. And that is why the honest form of writing about it is to say plainly what the record holds and what it does not. The saliva mark on the ground faded; the child came back before it did; the mother said the word. Everything else we know by testimony, and testimony is what a small community has instead of a written archive.

*Nwaezinmadu* asserts that a good name outlasts money. A record that gives the concept a false provenance in order to look complete has spent the one thing the concept is about. The archive's only useful contribution is to keep the attention going — to write down what was said, say who said it, and leave the rest of the ground as it was found, still wet.
