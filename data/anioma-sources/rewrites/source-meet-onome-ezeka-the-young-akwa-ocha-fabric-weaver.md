Meet Onome Ezeka, the Young Akwa-Ocha Fabric Weaver




By Emeka Esogbue



Locating Onocity City, her weaving mill around the Ite-Ego axis within the Ibusa community felt like finding a living home of Akwa-Ocha, the cherished cultural fabric of the Anioma people.




Inside the modest weaving space, the rhythmic sounds of the looms filled the air. Young trainees, eager to master the craft, moved about with enthusiasm, smiling, welcoming customers and visitors, and attending to orders. It was more than a weaving mill; it was a place where culture was being preserved, one thread at a time.




At the centre of it all is Onome Ezeka, a young, vibrant woman who has turned her passion for Akwa-Ocha into a growing enterprise and a platform for teaching others.



I first met Onome in 2016 when she contested for Miss Teen Anioma, during my time as the Publicity Person. I celebrated with her when she emerged as the first runner-up.




Years later, I would meet her again but this time, in a completely different setting. Today, Onome is deeply involved in the production of Akwa-Ocha, weaving not only fabric but also a story of passion, identity and cultural continuity.



When I asked her how she got into the craft, her response was simple:



“I have always loved Akwa-Ocha for its purity and what it represents. For this reason, I went on to learn how to produce it. Even the one I wore for the 2016 pageantry was produced by me.”



That revelation says much about Onome’s journey. What began as an appreciation for the beauty and meaning of Akwa-Ocha gradually became a skill, and eventually, a vocation.



Today, she says she is living a dream she nurtured for years.



“Now, I have my dream come true after many years because I now have a personal place of my own where I produce and also train people on the production of the Anioma cultural fabric,” Onome told Pen Master.



Her weaving mill is therefore more than a place of production. It is also a training ground for young people who are learning the intricate craft and, in the process, becoming part of the continuing story of Anioma’s cultural heritage.



Onome says her services are affordable and that her products are tastefully finished to suit the preferences and needs of her customers. According to her, this attention to quality and customer satisfaction is one of the reasons many customers return after their first experience.



For Onome, however, the work goes beyond business.



It is about preserving something precious.



It is about keeping the threads of Anioma identity firmly in the hands of a new generation.



And as her young trainees gather around the looms, learning to turn threads into the beautiful patterns of Akwa-Ocha, Onome Ezeka is quietly proving that culture does not have to live only in museums or memories.



Sometimes, it lives in the hands of a young woman who chooses to keep weaving.
