# Igbo Izugbe: The One Igbo a Dictionary Has to Choose

*For ozikoro.com — Cultural Heritage / Historical Studies. Draft by Ozikoro.*

---

Open the entry for **ike** on Ozituma and the page performs a small act of choice before the reader has read a word. The headword is written one way, in one variety of Igbo. Directly beneath it sit seven others — Ezaa, Mbaise, Ngwa, Nsa, Ẹkpẹyẹ, Ọnịcha, Ụmụahịa — each with its own spelling of the same word. One of them, Ọnịcha's, does not write *ike* at all. It writes *ume*.

That page is the subject of this piece, because the choice it makes is the choice every written dictionary of Igbo has to make, and it is not a neutral one.

## Igbo is not one speech

There is no single Igbo in the way there is a single French. The language is a continuum of varieties, and the number of them is not a matter of dispute so much as of method: count by town and the figure runs into the hundreds, count by the recognised clusters and it settles in the tens. Ozituma registers forty-eight.

A speaker from Nsụka and a speaker from Ọnịcha can hold a conversation and will still say several words differently, and some words one of them uses the other does not have. This is ordinary in a language spoken across a wide area for a long time. It becomes a problem at the moment someone decides to write it down.

## What izugbe means

Igbo **izugbe** is central Igbo — the word itself means *central* — and it is the variety that written Igbo settled on as its standard. It is the Igbo of schoolbooks, of most printed prose, of the news reader, and of the headword on a dictionary entry.

A standard variety of this kind is not the oldest form of a language, and it is not the purest. It is the form that was agreed on, largely for the practical reason that a written language has to be taught, examined, printed and indexed, and none of those things can be done in forty-eight directions at once.

## Why the headword has to be one variety

The decision looks arbitrary until you try to build the alternative.

**Search.** A reader types a word and expects to be found. If the headword could be any variety, the dictionary would need every spelling of every word as a heading, and the reader would have to guess which one the entry was filed under.

**Order.** A dictionary is an ordered object. If one entry is filed under a Ngwa spelling and the next under an Ọnịcha one, the alphabet stops meaning anything.

**Tone.** Igbo is a tonal language, and the tone of a word is part of the word. The standard supplies a settled set of conventions for marking it. A headword written without an agreed convention is not a spelling; it is a suggestion.

**Script.** Ozituma writes Igbo headwords in Ndebe, the syllabary, as well as in the Latin alphabet — 12,047 of its 12,232 Igbo entries. A syllabary cannot be applied to a spelling that has not been fixed, because the syllable boundaries depend on the spelling.

Every one of those requirements pushes in the same direction. The headword must be one variety, and it must be the standard.

## What happens to the others

Here the ordinary dictionary and a dictionary of Igbo part company.

Most dictionaries of a language with a strong standard quietly discard the rest. The other varieties are treated as corruptions of the written form, or as errors a speaker makes, and they are left out. That is a description of the standard's authority. It is not a description of the language.

Ozituma records the other varieties beside the headword rather than beneath it. On the entry for **ọrụ**, the Ajalị variety says *ihe ọmụmụ*; the Owere and Ọnịcha forms are recorded with theirs. Each carries its own recording where one exists, and each recording is attached to the spelling it is actually of — so a reader who plays the Ajalị form hears Ajalị, and never the standard. 46,189 recordings are published on the site on that principle.

The same reasoning runs through the proverbs. 1,880 proverbs are published with a meaning; where the recorded line is corrupt or fragmentary, the proverb is shown in Igbo alone and the English is left empty. A reading that cannot be established is not published with a warning attached, because a warning does not stop a reader from taking the reading.

## The varieties beside the headword

The seven varieties listed under *ike* are not a footnote. They are the record of how the word is actually said across the area where people say it.

That is what the standard cannot hold. A dictionary that writes only izugbe has written the language as it is taught, and has left out the language as it is spoken. Both are Igbo, and a record that keeps only one of them is accurate about the classroom and silent about the home.

The practical consequence is the one that matters most for anyone learning the language: a learner who goes to Nsụka carrying only the standard will be understood, and will still sound like a book. The varieties are what the dictionary has to give so that the learner can hear the difference between Igbo as it is written and Igbo as it is said where they are standing.

## Where the choice shows

The choice is visible in small places, which is how it should be. It is visible in the headword on every entry, in the tone marks, in the order of the list, and in the syllabary beneath the word. It is visible in the search box, which folds tone so that a reader who cannot type the marks is still found — *ulo* finds *ụlọ* — because the standard's precision must not become a barrier to reaching the record.

It is invisible in the place a reader would most expect to find it, which is the varieties section. There the dictionary stops choosing. Everything the record holds is shown, each with the name of the place it is said in.

## What a standard cannot do

A standard can be taught, printed, indexed and searched. It cannot be complete, and it cannot be the only Igbo. The varieties are not the standard's errors; they are the language's range, and the range is the older thing.

This is why the dictionary writes one Igbo at the top of the page and lists the rest beside it — not to rank them, but because a page has to start somewhere, and the honest version of that necessity is to show what was chosen and what was set next to it.
